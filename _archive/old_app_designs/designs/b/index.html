Tariff Talk

A quantitative analysis of every tariff-relevant Truth Social post from Trump's second term, matched to formal trade actions and indexed against the S&P 500.

Summary findings

  • ~26% of explicit tariff threats are modified or withdrawn before reaching their stated form — empirical support for the Wall Street acronym TACO ("Trump Always Chickens Out").
  • ~25% are executed as announced; the remainder land in investigations, struck-down rules, or unresolved limbo. The median lead time from threat to formal event is roughly 4 trading days.
  • The S&P 500's average 30-day cumulative abnormal return is near zero, but masks a wide path: an average peak drawdown of −4.5% and an average rebound of +3.5%.

Background: words that move markets

Since returning to office in January 2025, Donald Trump has used Truth Social as the de-facto press wire for U.S. trade policy. A post threatening 100% tariffs on Chinese semiconductors, or a 50% levy on the European Union, can move the S&P 500 within minutes. Some of these threats become Federal Register executive orders. Others are quietly walked back over a weekend phone call. Traders, watching this oscillation, coined a name for the pattern: TACO — Trump Always Chickens Out.

This essay asks three questions:

  1. Of all tariff-relevant posts, what share are credible commissive threats versus rhetorical bluster?
  2. How do markets price these threats in the days surrounding both the post and any matched policy event?
  3. Is there textual signal — particular hedging language — that predicts whether a threat will actually be executed?

We hand-labeled every tariff-relevant Truth Social post through the data cutoff, applied a speech-act taxonomy (commissive vs. directive vs. expressive), and matched each Class-A commissive economic threat to the PIIE Trade War Timeline. Market returns were computed against a market-model benchmark following MacKinlay (1997).

A threat is not a policy. The gap between the two — measured in days, dollars, and rhetorical hedges — is where this paper lives.

Section 1 · How we measure threats

We define a Class-A commissive economic threat as any Truth Social post in which the speaker (a) commits the United States to a future tariff, sanction, or trade action, (b) names or strongly implies a target, and (c) specifies either a rate or a triggering condition. Posts that merely complain about trade deficits, or that praise existing tariffs, are excluded.

Each qualifying threat is matched — by target, instrument, and date proximity — to the nearest PIIE-timeline event within a ±60-day window. The matched event determines the threat's realized outcome: executed, modified/withdrawn (TACO), struck down by a court, still announced-and-unresolved, opened as an investigation, or other.

Two facts stand out. First, only a quarter of threats reach the Federal Register in their originally-announced form. Second, the TACO bucket is roughly equal in size — a near-coin-flip between execution and reversal, with the residual probability mass scattered across investigations and pending status.

A note on matching

The data-generating process is messier than the categories suggest. A single threat (say, "200% tariff on EU wine") may map to a Section 301 investigation that is later folded into a broader reciprocal-tariff order. We collapse these chains into policy episodes, of which there are roughly seventy in the current dataset.

Section 2 · What happens after a threat

For each matched threat we compute the S&P 500's cumulative abnormal return over 1-, 5-, and 30-trading-day windows centered on the post timestamp. Abnormal returns are residuals from a market model estimated on a 120-day pre-event window — standard event-study mechanics (MacKinlay 1997).

CARi(τ₁,τ₂) = Σt=τ₁τ₂ (Ri,t − α̂i − β̂i Rm,t)

The headline statistic — an average 30-day CAR near zero — is misleading on its own. Within the same 30-day window, the average threat traces a peak drawdown of roughly −4.5% and a maximum rebound of +3.5%. The path is wider than the destination. For levered traders, the path is the trade.

Outcome conditions the return

Conditional on outcome, the picture diverges. Threats that are eventually withdrawn show markedly less negative 5-day returns than those executed as announced — consistent with traders partially anticipating the climb-down even before it materializes.

Section 3 · When does Trump fold?

Given that a threat is issued, two clocks start: a resolution clock (how many days until either execution or withdrawal) and a market clock (how long the implied volatility premium persists). We are interested in textual features of the original post that predict the resolution clock.

We fit a Cox proportional-hazards model treating "resolution" (executed OR withdrawn) as the event and pending threats as right-censored. Covariates include target country fixed effects, log post length, an explicit-rate indicator (does the post name a tariff percentage?), and the share of Loughran–McDonald hedging vocabulary in the post (Loughran & McDonald 2011).

h(t | x) = h₀(t) · exp( β₁ · hedge_share
                      + β₂ · has_rate
                      + β₃ · log_len
                      + γ · target_FE )

The headline coefficient: a one-unit increase in hedge-vocabulary share is associated with a hazard ratio of 8.6 (p = 0.003) — that is, posts heavy in words like may, could, possibly, perhaps, considering resolve substantially faster than posts written with categorical certainty. We flag this as preliminary: the sample is small, hedge tokens are sparse, and the result is sensitive to the choice of dictionary subset.

The interpretation is uncomfortable but tempting: when Trump posts in absolute terms ("Effective Monday: 100% tariff"), the post is doing policy work. When he hedges, the post is doing rhetorical work — and the rhetoric resolves more quickly because there is less to resolve.

What this does not prove

None of the above establishes that Trump's posts cause the market moves we measure. We cannot rule out reverse causation (posts timed to ongoing market narratives), confounding (simultaneous policy announcements by other parties), or omitted-variable bias from the broader news environment. A proper identification strategy would exploit high-frequency intraday data around the precise post timestamp — work we leave to a follow-up.
